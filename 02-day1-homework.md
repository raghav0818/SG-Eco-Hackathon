# Part 1 — Problem statement

## What I'm solving

Food waste in SAF cookhouses — specifically, **waste created by the portion decision** in pre-plated serveries, where the person eating has no way to influence how much food is put in front of them.

## The distinction the whole thing rests on

SAF cookhouses run in two configurations, and they fail for **opposite** reasons.

**Type 2 — served to order.** You queue with a tray, staff serve onto it, you can say "less rice." A mechanism already exists, it's free, and it costs one word.

**Type 1 — pre-plated heated display.** Staff portion food onto plates in advance. Plates sit under heat lamps. You take one and go. **There is no input mechanism at all.** You cannot ask a heated cabinet for less rice.

**I'm targeting Type 1, and I rejected Type 2 deliberately.**

My first instinct was that Type 2 was the target — that *friction* stops people asking for less, and that a device could lower it. That was wrong, for two independent reasons. Any device is strictly worse than speech: speaking costs one word and zero setup, a button adds a step. And state-tracking is unsolvable — if someone presses a button at the entrance, the server has no way to know which person in a moving queue pressed what, and fixing that needs an identity system I'm not willing to build. The real barrier in Type 2 isn't friction, it's the server's default scoop size. That makes the honest lever the ladle — a kitchen-side fix, and I've committed to intervening on the diner's side.

**Type 2 is a weak target precisely because the consumer-side solution already exists and works.**

## The two problems inside Type 1

**Problem A — one portion size for a population with roughly a 2× appetite range.** A pre-plated cookhouse serves one portion to everyone, from a small-framed clerk to a large infantryman post-exercise. Everyone who wanted less wastes the difference, every meal, **by design**. This waste is guaranteed by the architecture, not by carelessness. And it's invisible in aggregate — it never shows up as over-catering, because the food was correctly issued against entitlement. It only appears at the bin, where nobody measures it. The diner has no third option: eat too much, or scrape the plate.

**Problem B — plating is done blind, and plated food can't be recovered.** Staff plate against the indent — a forecast headcount — before actual turnout is known. Turnout moves with duty schedules, outfield, bookouts, weather. Forecasts are wrong. Plates unclaimed past hot-hold time are a **100% loss**: they can't be re-served, chilled and reissued, or donated. The loss is locked in at the moment of plating, hours before anyone knows.

**These interact.** If you solve A naively by offering two plate sizes without a demand signal, staff now have to guess the *mix* as well as the volume — and leftover plates of the unpopular size are pure waste. **Solving A without B relocates the waste instead of removing it.**

## How big

Roughly **60 cookhouses** nationwide, catering outsourced.

**The number a judge will find in 30 seconds, and my answer to it:** MINDEF has publicly stated SAF food waste is about **1% of meals catered** (Parliamentary Question, 3 Feb 2020), with waste going to biogas treatment at Ulu Pandan and Tuas Nexus.

That figure is real and I'm not disputing it. But it's a **catering-side** number, generated by the electronic meal accounting system — meals *issued* versus meals *drawn*. It measures over-catering. It says nothing about the portion decision, and nothing about food that was correctly issued, correctly drawn, and then left on the plate. Problem A produces waste that is invisible to that metric **by construction**.

**The 1% is evidence that the catering side is already well optimised — which is exactly why the remaining waste sits at the portion decision.**

For scale on where waste actually sits: a US EPA study at Fort Jackson (2019) instrumented two Army dining facilities and found **83% of food waste came from overproduction**, not plate scrapings — 27,000 lbs weighed over three months. The waste is upstream of the diner's fork.

## Who the target user is

**The diner in a Type 1 cookhouse** — a serviceman with no available action other than eating too much or binning the difference.

## Who else is involved

- **Cookhouse staff** — plating blind against a forecast, and never told what came back.
- **The two catering contractors**, SATS Defence Catering and Foodfare. Food waste is a direct cost against contract margin, so the commercial incentive is aligned without appealing to sustainability. **I don't have to convince the SAF unit by unit. I have to convince two companies.**
- **SAF Sustainability Office**, established 2020 — the policy channel.

## Why I picked it

I've eaten in these cookhouses. The Type 1 / Type 2 split isn't something I read — it's first-hand, and it's the part no competing team is likely to have.

And the environment forces an unusual design. **SAF cookhouses are Green Zone: unauthorised photography is prohibited and is an offence.** That has consequences most people would treat as blockers:

- I cannot photograph food waste in camp. Nobody can.
- A camera-based system — the obvious modern approach, and what most teams will reach for — is close to **undeployable** there. Not because it doesn't work, but because it would never clear a security review.
- So my evidence base is structured interviews, and my prototype senses **weight**, not images.

**Weight sensing answers every question a camera would, with no security review, no privacy question, and no personal data.** That isn't a shortcut around a hard problem — it's the only thing that survives the environment.

## What I still want to learn

Interview requests are out. Open questions: whether Type 1 plates already come in any size variation today (if they do, my main idea collapses); whether plates are routinely left at window close and what happens to them; whether seconds are genuinely available; and which servery type dominates, by meal and by camp.

Also worth acknowledging rather than hiding: newer cookhouses are moving to self-serve hawker-style formats, which changes the portion mechanism again.

## How this differs from what already exists

Winnow, Leanpath, Orbisk, Kitro — all camera-plus-scale over the **kitchen bin**, all chef-facing, all subscription-priced. None touch the portion decision. Raccoon Eyes (Georgia Tech, 500,000+ plates) is the closest — it shows students their own waste, but **at the bin, after the portion is already fixed**.

> **Every system built so far measures food waste after the portion is already decided — in the kitchen, or at the bin. In a pre-plated cookhouse the diner has no way to influence that portion at all, and the kitchen decides it blind.**

# Part 2 — Prioritised ideas

**Proposal, not a decision.** All three share one constraint set: no camera, no identity, no accounts, no personal data, zero added queue time, no training for diner or server, survives steam and grease, buildable for ≤S$200 on a Raspberry Pi.

## Idea 1 (primary) — Portion choice plus a self-correcting demand signal

**The concept.** Two plate sizes in the heated display, with a load cell under each shelf. The diner picks the plate they want — **the choice is the action**, nothing to press, say, or learn. Shelf weight divided by average plate weight gives plates remaining; the rate of change gives the take rate per size. That feeds a small display telling staff what mix to plate next.

`REGULAR 18 left · 6.0/min | SMALL 7 left · 4.2/min → plate 10 SMALL next | window closes 13:30`

**Why both halves together.** Plate sizes alone solve Problem A and hand the kitchen a harder guessing problem — that's the relocation trap. The demand signal is what closes the loop: **the servery measures its own demand instead of forecasting it.**

**What I'd build.** Raspberry Pi, 2× HX711 + 20 kg load cells, a shelf platform, and a display. Calibration is a physical routine rather than an assumption — place the shelf empty, press, save — because load cells drift and plates differ between camps.

**Honest risks.** Two plate sizes is not itself novel; "smaller plates reduce intake" is well established and I'm not claiming it as the innovation. The innovation is the closed loop. And this depends on Type 1 plates being uniform today, which interviews will tell me.

**The claim, in one sentence:** *offering portion choice in a system that structurally has none, plus a self-correcting demand signal so the kitchen plates the right mix without guessing — with no camera and no personal data, in an environment where a camera isn't deployable.*

## Idea 2 (add-on) — Seconds-availability display

`SMALL — 7 left · seconds until 13:30`

**The hypothesis:** people may take the large portion not from appetite but from **scarcity anxiety** — choosing small and still being hungry leaves no recourse. Showing that the recourse exists removes the downside of choosing less.

Runs on the **same hardware as Idea 1 at zero extra cost**, which is why it's an add-on rather than a competitor. Entirely dependent on whether seconds are actually permitted — an open interview question.

## Idea 3 (distinct second concept) — Dish-level waste feedback

*"6.2 kg of this came back yesterday."* Dish-specific, shown **at the dish, at the moment of choosing** — not on a poster by the door. Data comes from a daily bin weigh-in; no live sensor needed.

**Why it's on the list:** different mechanism, different hardware, no load cells — the strongest candidate if the second S$200 is unlocked. The behavioural literature is clear that **feedback resolution** is what matters: dorm-vs-dorm real-time feedback produced 55% reduction versus 31% for weekly.

**Honest risk:** information is the weakest lever available, and habituation within about a week is likely. Strong as a second prototype, weak as the only one.

## Control arm — trayless, S$0

Trayless dining is the best-established intervention there is: **20–32% reduction**, with Aramark measuring 25–30% across 186,000 meals at 25 campuses. Zero technology.

It doesn't apply to Type 1 (no tray), but I'll run it as a **free comparison arm at the test site** — testing against the best known alternative rather than against nothing.

## Testing, and what I won't claim

Camp deployment is impossible during the programme — Green Zone. The rig runs at the **SUTD canteen as a proxy servery**: photographable, baseline-able, iterable daily. I'll frame that honestly — tested in a proxy setting, target deployment is a cookhouse, here's what interviews say about the difference.

**Two weeks at n≈100 will not produce a statistically significant reduction, and I won't claim one.** I'm designing for uptake measurement, a clean baseline, and a comparison against a known alternative. A straight negative result reported honestly beats an inflated positive.

## Deliberately ruled out

| Idea | Why |
|---|---|
| Gamified app / leaderboard | Weakest category in the literature; cited results measure engagement, not reduction |
| ML tray scanner / camera vision | Raccoon Eyes did it at 500k+ plates — and a camera is undeployable in a Green Zone |
| Demand forecasting model | Crowded lane, and it's kitchen-side |
| Pre-commitment button at the entrance | Server can't tell who pressed what; needs identity. And speech is already faster |
| Tray clip / RFID token | Same tracking failure, plus it adds a step to something that costs one word |
| Anything requiring billing | Korea's pay-as-you-throw works, but no billing relationship exists with a serviceman |
